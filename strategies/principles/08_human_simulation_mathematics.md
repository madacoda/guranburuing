# Principle 08: Mathematical Models of Human Motor Simulation

## 1. Executive Summary

Server-side anti-cheat telemetry detects automation primarily by performing statistical entropy and variance tests on client input streams. In human biomechanics, neuromuscular motor control is governed by physical mass, muscle activation latency, and visual feedback loops. 

Robotic automation that teleports cursors, clicks exact pixel centers, or applies fixed sleep intervals exhibits **near-zero statistical entropy**, making detection trivial.

This document formalizes the mathematical and kinematic models required to synthesize realistic human motor behavior across three dimensions: **Trajectory Kinematics**, **Spatial Coordinate Sampling**, and **Temporal Latency Distributions**.

## 2. Trajectory Kinematics & The Minimum Jerk Model

### 2.1 The Biological Basis: Flash & Hogan Minimum Jerk Hypothesis
In human motor neuroscience (Flash & Hogan, 1985), voluntary smooth reaching movements minimize the time integral of the square of the jerk (the third derivative of position):

$$\mathcal{J} = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \left[ \left( \frac{d^3 x}{dt^3} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{d^3 y}{dt^3} \right)^2 \right] dt$$

Minimizing this cost function yields a smooth, 5th-order polynomial position trajectory:

$$x(\tau) = x_0 + (x_f - x_0) \left( 10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5 \right), \quad \tau = \frac{t}{T} \in [0, 1]$$

This produces the characteristic **symmetric bell-shaped velocity profile**:

$$v(\tau) = \frac{D}{T} \left( 30\tau^2 - 60\tau^3 + 30\tau^4 \right)$$

```
 Velocity v(t)
    ^
    |          .---.          <-- Peak velocity at t = 0.5 T
    |        /       \
    |       /         \
    |     /             \
    +----+---------------+----> Time (t)
    0                   T
```

### 2.2 Cubic Bézier Spline Approximation with Perpendicular Offset
To emulate natural human wrist arcs without excessive computational cost, trajectories are parameterized as **Cubic Bézier Curves**:

$$\mathbf{B}(t) = (1-t)^3 \mathbf{P}_0 + 3(1-t)^2 t \mathbf{P}_1 + 3(1-t) t^2 \mathbf{P}_2 + t^3 \mathbf{P}_3, \quad t \in [0, 1]$$

Where:
* $\mathbf{P}_0 = (x_0, y_0)$: Initial cursor position.
* $\mathbf{P}_3 = (x_f, y_f)$: Target element coordinate.
* $\mathbf{P}_1, \mathbf{P}_2$: Intermediate control points generated by displacing along the perpendicular unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\perp}$:

$$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{P}_3 - \mathbf{P}_0, \quad \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\perp} = \begin{bmatrix} -d_y / \|\mathbf{d}\| \\ d_x / \|\mathbf{d}\| \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_1 = \mathbf{P}_0 + \alpha_1 \mathbf{d} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\perp}) \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\perp}, \quad \alpha_1 \in [0.2, 0.4]$$

$$\mathbf{P}_2 = \mathbf{P}_0 + \alpha_2 \mathbf{d} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\perp}) \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\perp}, \quad \alpha_2 \in [0.6, 0.8]$$

## 3. Spatial Coordinate Sampling: 2D Bivariate Gaussian

Clicking the exact center $(x_c, y_c)$ of an element produces a delta distribution $\delta(x - x_c, y - y_c)$ that immediately fails spatial uniformity tests.

### 3.1 Bivariate Normal Distribution with Safety Zone Clamping
Clicks are sampled from a 2D Gaussian centered at the element's bounding box centroid:

$$\begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{bmatrix} x_c \\ y_c \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_x^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_y^2 \end{bmatrix} \right)$$

To ensure clicks remain well within the clickable area and avoid edge misses:
* $\sigma_x = \frac{\text{width}}{6}$
* $\sigma_y = \frac{\text{height}}{6}$
* Safe Clamping:

$$x_{\text{click}} = \max\left(x_{\min} + \delta_{\text{pad}}, \min\left(x_{\max} - \delta_{\pad}, X\right)\right)$$

$$y_{\text{click}} = \max\left(y_{\min} + \delta_{\text{pad}}, \min\left(y_{\max} - \delta_{\pad}, Y\right)\right)$$

Where $\delta_{\text{pad}} = 3\text{px}$ to prevent border clipping.

## 4. Temporal Latency Modeling: Log-Normal Distributions

Human reaction times and inter-action delays do not follow uniform distributions $\mathcal{U}(a, b)$ or fixed constants. They strictly follow **Log-Normal Distributions**:

$$f(t; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{t \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(\ln t - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \quad t > 0$$

```
 Probability Density
    ^
    |    *
    |   * *
    |  *   *
    | *     *
    |*       * * * * . . . .   (Long human tail)
    +-------------------------> Time Delay (ms)
```

## 5. Fitts's Law for Movement Duration Estimation

To calculate realistic movement duration $T$ from cursor distance $D$ and target width $W$:

$$T = a + b \log_2\left( \frac{D}{W} + 1 \right)$$

* $a \approx 120\text{ms}$ (Neuromuscular motor preparation latency)
* $b \approx 140\text{ms/bit}$ (Information processing index)
* For a typical 400px mouse movement to an 80px button:
  $$I_D = \log_2\left(\frac{400}{80} + 1\right) = \log_2(6) \approx 2.58 \text{ bits}$$
  $$T = 120 + 140 \times 2.58 \approx 481\text{ms}$$

This calculated duration $T$ is then used to parameterize the number of intermediate `mouseMoved` events dispatched via CDP.

## 6. Burst Clicking & Rapid Tapping Kinematics

In fast-paced raid environments (such as PBHL racing or Guild War bursts), human players exhibit physiological **rapid tapping** (double- or triple-clicking) when pressing abilities or confirming attacks while anticipating server response frames.

### 6.1 Neuromuscular Tremor & Tapping Frequency
Neuromuscular motor units in the human forearm (extensor digitorum and flexor digitorum profundus) achieve maximum tapping frequencies between 8 Hz and 14 Hz:

$$\Delta t_{\text{inter-tap}} \sim \mathcal{U}(50\text{ms}, 120\text{ms})$$

### 6.2 Micro-Displacement Spatial Drift
Successive rapid clicks on the same button do not land on the exact identical coordinate. Micro-tremors and finger compression introduce spatial drift between successive taps:

$$\mathbf{p}_{i+1} = \mathbf{p}_i + \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x_i \\ \Delta y_i \end{bmatrix}, \quad \Delta x_i, \Delta y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{jitter}}^2), \quad \sigma_{\text{jitter}} = 1.5\text{px}$$

Clamped within the element's bounding box padding $\delta_{\text{pad}} = 3\text{px}$.

### 6.3 Implementation in `human-motor.ts`
```typescript
export interface HumanClickOptions {
  allowMultiClick?: boolean;  // Enable natural player double/triple tapping
  multiClickChance?: number;  // Probability (e.g. 0.35 - 0.50)
  maxClicks?: number;         // Maximum successive taps (typically 2-3)
}
```

When `allowMultiClick` is enabled, the driver samples a Bernoulli trial with parameter $p = \text{multiClickChance}$. Upon success:
1. Dispatches the initial click at $(x_1, y_1)$.
2. Waits a rapid biological interval $\Delta t \in [65, 120]\text{ms}$.
3. Dispatches subsequent taps at $(x_1 + \Delta x, y_1 + \Delta y)$ with $\sigma_{\text{jitter}} = 1.5\text{px}$.

## 7. Physical Switch Actuation & Hover Latencies

Sub-millisecond mouse button clicks (instantaneous `mouseDown` immediately followed by `mouseUp` at $\Delta t < 15\text{ms}$) are a hallmark of naive synthetic automation. Human physical mouse actuation involves mechanical switch travel and spring resistance:

### 7.1 Actuation Hold Duration
Real mechanical mouse switches (Omron, Kailh, optical microswitches) require physical compression and release phases:

$$T_{\text{hold}} \sim \text{Log-Normal}(\mu = \ln(50\text{ms}), \sigma = 0.18), \quad T_{\text{hold}} \in [40\text{ms}, 75\text{ms}]$$

### 7.2 Pre-Click Hover & Post-Click Release
Before depressing the mouse switch, human fingers exhibit a subtle decelerating hover phase. Following release, a brief recovery latency occurs before initiating the next motor action:
* **Pre-Click Hover**: $T_{\text{pre}} \in [40\text{ms}, 75\text{ms}]$
* **Post-Click Release**: $T_{\text{post}} \in [45\text{ms}, 75\text{ms}]$

## 8. Cognitive Reaction Latency Modeling (`humanReactionDelay`)

Human players cannot initiate physical actions instantaneously when visual cues appear on screen (e.g. combat HUD unlocking, supporter list rendering, or skill drawers sliding open). Visual-motor reaction time comprises visual transduction, cortical decision-making, and peripheral nerve conduction:

$$T_{\text{reaction}} \sim \text{Log-Normal}(\mu = \ln(300\text{ms}), \sigma = 0.22)$$

```typescript
export async function humanReactionDelay(baseMs = 320, shape = 0.22): Promise<void> {
  await logNormalDelay(baseMs, shape);
}
```

This delay is injected immediately before interacting with dynamically rendered elements:
- Quick Summon button ready $\rightarrow$ $280\text{ms}$ pause $\rightarrow$ Click.
- Character skill panel rendered $\rightarrow$ $350\text{ms} - 450\text{ms}$ visual search $\rightarrow$ Click.
- Target selection popup $\rightarrow$ $380\text{ms}$ visual identification $\rightarrow$ Click.
- Combat HUD ready / Attack button unlock $\rightarrow$ $300\text{ms}$ perception latency $\rightarrow$ Click.

## 9. Domain Window Preservation & Anti-Collapse Scaling

In automation engines with variable speed profiles (`stealth`, `fast`, `turbo`), naive implementations frequently slash all delay durations by aggressive constant factors (e.g. `delay * 0.40`).

### 9.1 The Animation Collapse Hazard
In Granblue Fantasy, certain game animations (such as Death summon party sacrifices, ability cooldown lockouts, or supporter list loads) are governed by the server and client rendering engine, requiring minimum absolute windows (e.g. $1200\text{ms} - 1600\text{ms}$). Arbitrarily shrinking explicit delays causes race conditions where buttons are clicked before DOM readiness, resulting in selector timeouts.

### 9.2 Centroid-Sampled Gaussian Window (`randomDelay`)
To prevent window collapse while preserving human entropy, `randomDelay(minMs, maxMs)` samples from a normal distribution centered at the midpoint $t_{\text{mid}} = \frac{t_{\min} + t_{\max}}{2}$, clamping strictly within $[t_{\min}, t_{\max}]$:

$$T \sim \mathcal{N}\left(t_{\text{mid}}, \left(\frac{t_{\max} - t_{\min}}{5}\right)^2\right), \quad T \in [t_{\min}, t_{\max}]$$

Under the `fast` speed profile, durations are scaled conservatively to $75\% - 85\%$ of the sampled value, preserving animation buffers while maintaining swift execution.

## 10. Combat Pacing & Anticipatory F5 Refresh Kinematics

In competitive PBHL racing, human players skip lengthy combat attack animations via browser reload (`F5`). Human refresh execution exhibits distinct behavioral pacing:

1. **Anticipatory Refresh Trigger**: Rather than refreshing at $0\text{ms}$, players wait for the visual confirmation of the attack button depressing:
   $$T_{\text{refresh\_trigger}} \in [180\text{ms}, 380\text{ms}]$$
2. **Post-Reload Visual Orientation**: After the reload completes, players briefly observe the refreshed battlefield (evaluating remaining boss HP and combat HUD status) before initiating the next command:
   $$T_{\text{post\_reload}} \in [450\text{ms}, 750\text{ms}]$$
3. **Natural Micro-Hesitations (3%–5% probability)**: During continuous raid farming, players occasionally hesitate ($350\text{ms} - 800\text{ms}$) to glance at raid participants or chat, disrupting uniform algorithmic rhythms.
